\begin{abstract}
Extreme event attribution concerns the change in the probability of a
specified class of events under a change in climate. For regional heat
extremes, this problem requires both a statistical description of the
upper tail and a definition of the climate states being compared. We
consider an observational approach in which block maxima of regional
three-day temperatures follow a generalized extreme value distribution
with a location parameter depending on global mean surface temperature.
Within this framework, probability ratios, attributable fractions, and
return-level differences are distinct summaries of the same pair of
conditional distributions. Their estimation is examined with particular
attention to finite upper endpoints, uncertainty in tail extrapolation,
and the distinction between observed and return-period-defined thresholds.
A European heat case study illustrates how moderate changes in return
levels can accompany very large changes in exceedance probabilities.
Results from four daytime and two nighttime temperature products show
positive intensity changes across all fitted indices, substantial
cross-product differences in risk ratios, and FAR values approaching
one for several cooler-climate comparisons. The analysis connects these
patterns to fitted tail shape, finite endpoints, and bootstrap behavior.
\end{abstract}
\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} extreme event attribution; nonstationarity;
generalized extreme value distribution; heat extremes; risk ratio;
fraction of attributable risk.

\section{Introduction}
An unusually severe heatwave raises two related statistical questions:
how exceptional was the event in the climate in which it occurred, and
how would its probability have differed under another climate? The first
concerns the tail of a single distribution. The second requires a
comparison of distributions and a specification of the change separating
them. Extreme event attribution brings these questions together by
expressing the influence of climate change through changes in the
probability or intensity of a defined event class
\cite{stott2016,otto2017,naveau2020}.

The European summer of 2003 provided an influential early application of
this approach \cite{stott2004}. Subsequent studies have considered a wider
range of hazards, spatial scales, and experimental designs. Evidence for
changes in temperature extremes at larger scales \cite{kim2016} provides
an important context, but does not by itself determine the attribution of
a particular regional event. That attribution depends on the temperature
index, the period and region over which it is defined, and the climate
comparison. Even when motivated by a single observed episode, the
statistical target is generally a set of possible realizations rather
than the probability of reproducing its exact meteorological history.

The statistical difficulty is especially apparent when an event that is
rare in the warmer climate lies far into the tail of the cooler-climate
distribution. A small change in the fitted distribution may then produce
a large probability ratio. Direct empirical estimation requires enough
exceedances in both climates, whereas parametric estimation replaces
that requirement with assumptions about tail behavior. Extreme value
theory provides a basis for such extrapolation, but uncertainty about the
tail and the validity of the climate comparison remains central to the
result \cite{davison2015,naveau2020}. The apparent precision of a fitted
risk ratio therefore cannot be assessed independently of the data and
assumptions used to obtain it.

A further distinction concerns the source of the comparison. Numerical
climate experiments can represent different forcing scenarios, while a
historical observational record contains a transient sequence of climate
states. A regression of regional extremes on a climate covariate uses
that sequence to infer conditional distributions. This approach estimates how risk varies with the climate covariate
through a common statistical model. The present work focuses on those
observational distributional contrasts and their uncertainty. Its
climate-state comparisons form part of the broader attribution workflow
described by Philip et al.\ \cite{philip2020}.

This paper develops that observational formulation for European heat
extremes, using the case-study settings and implementation associated
with a World Weather Attribution report \cite{keeping2026}. The focus is
a nonstationary generalized extreme value (GEV) model for annual and June
maxima of three-day regional temperatures. Rather than surveying the full
range of attribution methods, we examine how event definition, climate
dependence, and tail estimation jointly determine its conclusions. The
basic probability setup is followed by the construction of the data and
covariates, statistical estimation, and uncertainty quantification. The results compare annual and June extremes across four daytime and
two nighttime temperature products, reporting probability ratios,
attributable fractions, intensity changes, and bootstrap intervals.

\section{Basic setup}
\label{sec:setup}
Let $X$ denote a scalar summary of a temperature field over a specified
region and time block. For a threshold $u$, the event of interest is
$\{X>u\}$. Write $F(x\mid g)$ for its cumulative distribution function at
climate state $g$, represented here by global mean surface temperature
(GMST). For a factual state $G_F$ and a cooler comparison state $G_C$,
define
\begin{equation}
p_F(u)=1-F(u\mid G_F),\qquad p_C(u)=1-F(u\mid G_C).
\label{eq:probabilities}
\end{equation}
Both probabilities refer to the same index and threshold. Their
comparison would change meaning if the event definition changed between
climates. The probability ratio and fraction of attributable risk are
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RR}(u)=\frac{p_F(u)}{p_C(u)},\qquad
\mathrm{FAR}(u)=1-\frac{p_C(u)}{p_F(u)},
\label{eq:rr}
\end{equation}
when the denominators are positive. RR is also commonly called the
probability ratio. These quantities summarize relative changes in risk under the
specified climate contrast. FAR is reported alongside RR because its
upper bound of one provides a complementary description of large relative
changes.

The notation describes a climate-state comparison, not a sampling
scheme. In a numerical experiment, the two distributions might be
estimated from separate ensembles. In the present analysis they are
evaluations of a single conditional model fitted to historical pairs
$(X_t,G_t)$. This construction links the distributions through a common
parameterization and allows a transient record to inform comparisons at
fixed climate states. It also makes the results conditional on the form
of that relationship.

\subsection{Event definition}
The European case study concerns the region
$40$--$60^{\circ}$N and $10.5^{\circ}$W--$20^{\circ}$E. Let
$T^{\max}_{d,i}$ and $T^{\min}_{d,i}$ denote daily maximum and minimum
temperature on day $d$ at grid cell $i$. The daily temperatures are first
averaged over the selected land footprint, then averaged over three
consecutive days, and finally maximized within an annual or June block.
The resulting indices are denoted Tx3x and Tn3x, respectively. Tn3x thus
characterizes persistent warm nights through maxima of daily minimum
temperature.

This reduction of a temperature field to a scalar variable is part of
the statistical problem. A maximum of regional averages identifies a
spatially coherent regional episode, whereas an average of local maxima
may combine peaks occurring on different dates. Similarly, annual and
June maxima describe different event populations. Each yields one
observation per year, but the latter restricts the possible episodes to
June. As discussed by Naveau et al.\ \cite{naveau2020} and van Oldenborgh
et al.\ \cite{van2021}, choices of spatial extent and duration affect both
rarity and the estimated climate signal. A domain selected after
inspecting an exceptional realization also raises a selection issue
that ordinary parameter intervals do not resolve.

The threshold can be supplied by an observed value $x_{\mathrm{obs}}$ or
specified indirectly through a factual return period $T>1$. In the
second formulation,
\begin{equation}
x_F(T)=F^{-1}(1-1/T\mid G_F),\qquad p_F\{x_F(T)\}=1/T.
\label{eq:threshold}
\end{equation}
The implementation uses the return periods in Table~\ref{tab:events}.
They define the reference event classes used in this analysis; they are
not intrinsic characteristics of Tx3x or Tn3x. With an observed
threshold, both probabilities in Equation~\eqref{eq:probabilities} must
instead be estimated. The distinction affects the uncertainty analysis
because a fitted quantile changes when the fitted distribution changes,
whereas a specified observed threshold remains fixed.

\subsection{Climate comparisons and conditioning}
The case study compares the factual climate with states characterized
by GMST decrements of $0.6$ and $1.1\ ^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$:
\begin{equation}
G_C=G_F-\Delta G,\qquad
\Delta G\in\{0.6,1.1\}\ ^{\circ}\mathrm{C}.
\label{eq:climate}
\end{equation}
Following the motivating report \cite{keeping2026}, these are described
as 2003-like and 1976-like climates. The years identify historical
reference states; their realized weather is not used as a pair of
observations to be subtracted. Nor does either decrement represent the
removal of all anthropogenic forcing. Where the term counterfactual is
used below, it refers to the specified cooler comparison within the
fitted model.

The analysis is conditional on GMST but does not hold the circulation
of the motivating heatwave fixed. Consequently, its event probabilities
refer to regional heat episodes across the weather variability
represented by the model. A comparison conditioned on a particular
circulation trajectory would answer a different question
\cite{shepherd2016}. This distinction matters because a change in the
frequency of circulation states and a change in temperature within a
circulation state can both contribute to a change in heat risk.

\section{Temperature data and climate covariates}
\label{sec:data}
\subsection{Observational products and spatial support}
The implementation draws on four gridded temperature products
(Table~\ref{tab:data}). CPC and E-OBS are principally station-based
gridded analyses, Berkeley Earth provides an observation-based
reconstruction, and ERA5 is a reanalysis combining observations with a
numerical weather model. These products differ in temporal coverage,
spatial representation, and the way observations enter the analysis.
They may also share observational inputs. Agreement across products is
therefore informative about sensitivity to data construction, but does
not provide independent replication in a simple sampling sense.

For a regular longitude--latitude grid, area weighting is represented
by $w_i=\cos(\phi_i)$ within the chosen footprint and zero outside it.
With $M_{d,i}$ indicating an available observation, the daily regional
mean and its spatial coverage are
\begin{equation}
\overline T_d=
\frac{\sum_i w_iM_{d,i}T_{d,i}}{\sum_i w_iM_{d,i}},\qquad
c_d=\frac{\sum_i w_iM_{d,i}}{\sum_i w_i}.
\label{eq:spatial}
\end{equation}
Actual cell areas replace cosine weights on irregular grids. A regional
mean is retained when $c_d\geq0.95$. The footprint is fixed across dates,
although the subset contributing to a retained mean can still vary with
missingness. For CPC, E-OBS, and Berkeley Earth, the implementation
constructs this footprint from cells available on at least 95\% of input
dates. ERA5 uses a mapped E-OBS footprint as an approximate land mask.
These rules define the effective spatial support of the index and are
therefore relevant to comparisons among products.

\subsection{Temporal aggregation}
For either daily temperature variable, the regional three-day mean and
block maximum are
\begin{equation}
A_d=\frac{\overline T_{d-2}+\overline T_{d-1}+\overline T_d}{3},
\qquad X_t=\max_{d\in\mathcal D_t}A_d,
\label{eq:index}
\end{equation}
where $\mathcal D_t$ contains only endpoints of windows lying entirely
within the selected block. Thus June windows begin with 1--3 June,
and annual windows do not span two calendar years. Dates absent from
the input must be represented explicitly as missing; adjacency in a
data array alone does not establish that observations are consecutive.
Temperature units and calendar conventions likewise enter the definition
of the resulting series.

A three-day window is omitted if any of its components is missing, and
a block is retained only if at least 95\% of its regional daily values
are available. Such a coverage rule limits data loss but does not make
missingness ignorable for extremes. Missing a short hot spell can lower
the recorded block maximum even when overall coverage is high. This
source of observational uncertainty is distinct from the sampling
uncertainty of a GEV model fitted to the retained maxima.

\subsection{Representation of the climate state}
The climate covariate is constructed from NASA GISTEMP monthly global
land--ocean temperature anomalies relative to 1951--1980. Let $g_t$ be
the mean of twelve complete monthly values in year $t$. The implementation
uses the smoothed series
\begin{equation}
G_t=\frac{g_{t-2}+g_{t-1}+g_t+g_{t+1}}{4}.
\label{eq:gmst}
\end{equation}
This covariate summarizes variation on a longer timescale than the
individual regional heat episode. Its use as a predictor does not,
however, imply that all variability shared by regional extremes and
GMST is attributable to one external forcing.

Equation~\eqref{eq:gmst} requires the following year's anomaly and is
therefore a retrospective smoother. At the endpoint, the implementation
extrapolates unavailable annual values using a trend fitted over
2011--2025, obtaining $G_F\approx1.2431\ ^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$ for 2026.
This number is an estimated smoothed anomaly relative to the GISTEMP
baseline, rather than a temperature change relative to preindustrial
conditions. Historical fitting excludes 2026. Because the factual
distribution is evaluated at an estimated endpoint covariate, alternative
smoothing and extrapolation choices form part of the sensitivity analysis.

\section{Nonstationary extreme value modeling}
\label{sec:model}
\subsection{Tail modeling and block maxima}
For a stationary sample, an exceedance probability can be estimated by
the fraction of observations above a threshold. Its usefulness depends
on the number of exceedances: a short series provides little direct
information about probabilities much smaller than the reciprocal of
its length. In the present setting, observations also arise under
different climate states, so pooling their exceedances would not estimate
a fixed-climate probability. Parametric modeling addresses these two
issues by describing tail behavior and its dependence on the covariate.

Extreme value theory motivates the GEV family as a limiting model for
suitably normalized block maxima \cite{coles2001,davison2015}. Its
cumulative distribution function is
\begin{equation}
F(x;\mu,\sigma,\xi)=
\exp\left\{-\left[1+\xi\frac{x-\mu}{\sigma}\right]^{-1/\xi}\right\},
\quad \sigma>0,\quad 1+\xi(x-\mu)/\sigma>0.
\label{eq:gev}
\end{equation}
The limit at $\xi=0$ is the Gumbel distribution,
$F(x)=\exp\{-\exp[-(x-\mu)/\sigma]\}$. The location and scale parameters
control position and spread, while the shape parameter determines the
upper-tail form. In particular, $\xi<0$ implies a finite upper endpoint
$\mu-\sigma/\xi$. The limiting argument motivates a family of models;
it does not guarantee that a finite annual or June maximum follows that
family exactly. The adequacy of the approximation remains empirical.

\subsection{Climate dependence and likelihood}
We represent the historical sequence by
\begin{equation}
X_t\mid G_t\sim\mathrm{GEV}(\mu_t,\sigma,\xi),\qquad
\mu_t=\mu_0+\alpha(G_t-\overline G),
\label{eq:model}
\end{equation}
where $\overline G$ is the mean covariate over the fitting period.
Centering makes $\mu_0$ the location at the reference climate and leaves
contrasts between climate states unchanged. The slope $\alpha$ is
measured in degrees of regional extreme temperature per degree of GMST.
Annual and June indices, and daytime and nighttime temperatures, are
modeled separately.

Constant scale and shape impose a location shift: all quantiles respond
to GMST by the same amount. This restriction gives a parsimonious
representation for a limited record, but excludes changes in variability
or tail shape. In particular, a conclusion that intensity change is
independent of return period follows from this assumption rather than
constituting separate evidence for it. A model with changing scale would
allow a different response at different quantiles, at the cost of
estimating additional parameters from relatively few maxima.

Under conditional independence across blocks, inference is based on
\begin{equation}
\ell(\theta)=\sum_t\log f(X_t;\mu_t,\sigma,\xi),
\qquad \theta=(\mu_0,\alpha,\sigma,\xi),
\label{eq:likelihood}
\end{equation}
with all observations constrained to their year-specific support. The
existing implementation optimizes log scale, uses multiple starting
values for shape, and retains the best converged likelihood. The trend
coefficient is not constrained to be positive. Fits with $\xi\leq-0.5$
are rejected because standard likelihood regularity is problematic in
that range. This restriction is an estimation choice and should be
reported when interpreting fitted endpoints or failed bootstrap samples.

\subsection{Assessment of the fitted distribution}
The probability integral transform provides a common diagnostic scale:
\begin{equation}
u_t=F(X_t;\widehat\mu_t,\widehat\sigma,\widehat\xi),
\qquad z_t=\Phi^{-1}(u_t).
\end{equation}
With known parameters and a correct continuous model, the transforms
would be uniform and standard normal, respectively. Estimated parameters
alter their sampling behavior. Quantile plots and residual patterns can
nevertheless reveal departures in distributional shape, remaining
covariate dependence, or changing spread; serial dependence challenges
the factorization in Equation~\eqref{eq:likelihood}. Simulating and
refitting the model allows diagnostic envelopes to reflect estimation.

Diagnostics are especially consequential for attribution because the
cooler-climate probability may be far smaller than probabilities directly
represented in the data. A satisfactory fit over the observed range is
necessary evidence for the model, but provides limited validation of
such remote extrapolation. The existing ERA5 annual diagnostic exercise
indicates departures requiring further investigation. Its estimates, together with the other provisional product results,
are therefore interpreted conditional on the fitted model and the
remaining diagnostic limitations.

\section{Probability and intensity contrasts}
\label{sec:contrasts}
\subsection{Return levels and climate-specific risk}
Evaluating Equation~\eqref{eq:model} at $G_F$ and $G_C$ gives locations
$\mu_F$ and $\mu_C$. For $\xi\neq0$, the return level at either state is
\begin{equation}
x_j(T)=\mu_j+\frac{\sigma}{\xi}
\left\{[-\log(1-1/T)]^{-\xi}-1\right\},\qquad j\in\{F,C\}.
\label{eq:return}
\end{equation}
At $\xi=0$, it is
$x_j(T)=\mu_j-\sigma\log[-\log(1-1/T)]$. The return period is the
reciprocal of the one-block exceedance probability at the stated
climate. Under nonstationarity it is not a forecast of a constant
waiting time over future years, since those years may have different
exceedance probabilities.

For the return-period-defined event, both distributions are evaluated at
the same factual threshold $x_F(T)$:
\begin{equation}
p_F=1/T,\qquad p_C=1-F\{x_F(T);\mu_C,\sigma,\xi\}.
\end{equation}
If $p_F=0.04$ and $p_C=0.01$, the risk ratio is four and FAR is 0.75.
The latter expresses a relative difference in event probabilities, not
a fraction of temperature. For an observed event, replacing $x_F(T)$
with $x_{\mathrm{obs}}$ changes the estimand: the factual probability is
then estimated rather than fixed by construction.

\subsection{Intensity at a common probability}
A complementary comparison fixes the exceedance probability and
compares the associated temperatures. Under the location-shift model,
\begin{equation}
\Delta I(T)=x_F(T)-x_C(T)=\mu_F-\mu_C
=\alpha(G_F-G_C)=\alpha\Delta G.
\label{eq:intensity}
\end{equation}
The scale and shape terms cancel, so the intensity contrast is constant
across return periods. Risk ratios generally retain a dependence on the
threshold because the same horizontal shift can have different effects
at different positions in the tail. Thus a moderate and relatively
stable intensity contrast can coexist with a very large and uncertain
risk ratio. They answer different questions and need not have similar
statistical precision.

\subsection{Support and extreme probability ratios}
For negative shape, the cooler-climate upper endpoint is
$x_C^+=\mu_C-\sigma/\xi$. If $x_F(T)\geq x_C^+$, the fitted model assigns
zero probability to exceedance in that climate and hence an infinite
risk ratio. This is a consequence of the estimated support, not direct
evidence that the event is physically impossible. Sampling uncertainty
in $\xi$, model misspecification, and the climate-dependence assumption
all affect this conclusion.

Even within the fitted support, a very small $p_C$ makes the ratio
sensitive to small changes in tail parameters. This behavior is
substantive, but can be compounded by numerical cancellation if survival
probabilities are obtained by subtracting a cumulative probability close
to one. The implementation evaluates the survival probability in
logarithmic form and uses
$\log\mathrm{RR}=-\log T-\log p_C$. A zero induced by fitted support
must be distinguished from numerical underflow; replacing either by an
arbitrary small constant would impose an artificial upper bound on RR.

\section{Uncertainty quantification}
\label{sec:uncertainty}
\subsection{Conditional parameter uncertainty}
Because both climate distributions depend on the same fitted parameters,
the uncertainty of their contrast must preserve this dependence. The
implementation uses a parametric bootstrap conditional on the observed
years and GMST covariates. For replicate $b$, it simulates
\begin{equation}
X_t^{(b)}\sim
\mathrm{GEV}(\widehat\mu_t,\widehat\sigma,\widehat\xi),
\end{equation}
refits the model, and computes the attribution quantities from the
replicate parameters. In a return-period-defined analysis, the factual
threshold $x_F^{(b)}(T)$ is recomputed in each replicate. For a specified
observed threshold, $x_{\mathrm{obs}}$ is held fixed. Using one convention
for the point estimate and the other for resampling would quantify
uncertainty for a different target.

Empirical 2.5th and 97.5th percentiles give nominal 95\% percentile
intervals under this procedure. Their finite-sample performance is not
guaranteed, particularly when a risk ratio is influenced by a support
boundary. Paciorek et al.\ \cite{paciorek2018} compare interval methods
for attribution and show that commonly used bootstrap methods can be
outperformed in some settings. Their findings motivate care in choosing
and assessing an interval procedure, rather than a general claim that
bootstrap intervals have reliable coverage for all extreme-value fits.

The existing example uses 200 requested replicates, which is a modest
basis for estimating tail percentiles. Monte Carlo variation, failed
fits, and infinite ratios need to be distinguished from sampling
uncertainty. In particular, deleting infinite ratios changes the
estimated uncertainty distribution, while retaining only successful
fits may condition on a nonrepresentative subset of simulated samples.
Reported intervals should therefore be accompanied by the number and
nature of failed fits and an assessment of endpoint stability. Similarly,
pointwise return-level intervals do not constitute a simultaneous band
for an entire curve.

\subsection{Other sources of uncertainty}
The bootstrap varies the fitted GEV parameters while holding the product,
footprint, event definition, covariates, and model family fixed. It does
not propagate the full uncertainty associated with constructing those
inputs. As emphasized in broader statistical treatments of attribution
\cite{naveau2020}, observational error, internal variability, finite
sampling, and structural model error are related but distinct sources.
Increasing the number of bootstrap replicates reduces simulation error;
it does not resolve a misspecified tail model or an uncertain climate
comparison.

In the European application, sensitivity to the fitting period can be
separated partly from sensitivity to the temperature product by also
comparing products over common years. Alternative masks probe the
spatial definition, and alternative endpoint treatments probe the GMST
representation. Allowing scale to depend on climate examines a central
restriction behind Equation~\eqref{eq:intensity}. These comparisons
provide evidence about robustness, although a collection of sensitivity
results is not itself a calibrated probability distribution over model
uncertainty.

\section{Results: European extreme heat}
\label{sec:example}
\subsection{Data coverage and fitted distributions}
The results comprise 12 fitted distributions and 24 climate contrasts:
annual and June Tx3x for ERA5, CPC, Berkeley Earth, and E-OBS, and annual
and June Tn3x for CPC and E-OBS. The outputs are labeled provisional because
the data versions and implementation choices are not identical to those
of the motivating report. The present comparisons retain each product's
available fitting period rather than refitting to a common period.
Consequently, differences among estimates reflect both product
construction and record coverage. No pooled estimate is calculated.

Table~\ref{tab:fits} gives the sample sizes, thresholds, and fitted
parameters. CPC contributes 47 maxima per block, ERA5 contributes 76,
and E-OBS contributes 106. Berkeley Earth contributes 127 annual and 128
June maxima. All 12 fitted location slopes are positive. For daytime
extremes, annual slopes range from 1.90 to 3.67 degrees of regional
temperature per degree of GMST; June slopes range from 2.22 to 3.94.
The nighttime slopes are smaller within each of the two products, and
June slopes exceed annual slopes for both temperature indices in every
product where the comparison is available.

The tail parameters show a different pattern. CPC annual Tx3x has
$\widehat\xi=0.193$, whereas the other 11 fitted distributions have
negative shape. This difference is important for comparing risk ratios:
a large location shift need not give the largest relative risk increase
when the fitted distributions have different tail behavior. Each
product also defines its own factual quantile threshold. The annual
Tx3x thresholds range from $30.90$ to $33.18\ ^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$;
cross-product comparisons therefore concern equal factual return periods,
not a common absolute temperature threshold.

\subsection{Annual daytime extremes}
Table~\ref{tab:annualtx} reports RR, FAR, and intensity change for annual
Tx3x at a factual 25-year threshold. Relative to the 2003-like climate,
RR ranges from 3.74 for CPC to 37.1 for ERA5, corresponding to FAR values
of 0.73273 and 0.97306. Berkeley Earth and E-OBS give intermediate RRs
of 9.53 and 19.4, with FAR values of 0.89503 and 0.94839. Thus the
estimated fraction of factual risk associated with this climate contrast
ranges from about 73.3\% to 97.3\% across products.

The 1976-like comparison increases all four probability ratios, but by
very different amounts: 9.15 for CPC, 195 for Berkeley Earth,
$2.08\times10^3$ for E-OBS, and $4.02\times10^4$ for ERA5. The
corresponding FAR values are 0.89073, 0.99487, 0.99952, and 0.99998.
FAR compresses differences once RR is large, so values near one should
be read together with the underlying ratios and intervals. The
five-decimal display is descriptive precision, not a claim that FAR is
known to that accuracy. Finite-RR point estimates that would round to
1.00000 are displayed as $>0.99999$ in the results tables.

\subsection{Return-level curves and cross-product comparisons}
Figure~\ref{fig:returnlevels} displays the saved annual Tx3x return-level
curves for the factual and 1976-like climates. The fixed vertical
separation within each panel follows from the location-shift model.
Historical maxima have been adjusted to the respective climate state,
$G_*$, using
\begin{equation}
X_t^{(*)}=X_t+\widehat\alpha(G_*-G_t).
\end{equation}
The plotted points are therefore transformed observations, not separate
observational samples from the two climates. The figure's pointwise
bands use a separate set of 300 parametric bootstrap fits per product,
all successful, whereas the attribution tables use 200 requested fits.
In this figure, red curves denote the factual climate and blue curves the 1976-like
climate. Points are climate-adjusted historical maxima; shaded regions
are pointwise 95\% parametric-bootstrap intervals from 300 replicates
per product. The magenta line marks each product's factual 25-year
threshold. Vertical scales differ across panels; bands extending beyond
the panel limits are clipped in the original plot.

\subsection{Bootstrap behavior and precision}
The retained bootstrap counts in Table~\ref{tab:bootstrap} range from
178 to 200 out of 200 requested fits. All fits are retained for ERA5,
Berkeley Earth, and E-OBS, and for CPC annual Tx3x. CPC June Tx3x
retains 188, CPC annual Tn3x retains 199, and CPC June Tn3x retains
178. The last case therefore excludes 11\% of requested replicates.
The saved samples identify failed fits as missing but do not distinguish
the individual failure reasons. The reported intervals are conditional
on successful refitting.

Infinite bootstrap ratios are common even when the point estimate is
finite. For annual ERA5 Tx3x, 90 of 200 retained replicates give infinite
RR for the 1976-like contrast. For June CPC Tn3x, the corresponding
count is 171 of 178, or about 96.1\%. Its reported lower 95\% endpoint
is nevertheless finite, approximately $3.64\times10^5$, because a small
number of retained replicates have finite ratios. In contrast, annual
CPC Tx3x has only two infinite ratios among 200 replicates for the
1976-like comparison, so its reported 97.5th percentile remains finite
at approximately 6,352. These differences show why the number of
infinite replicates is informative alongside the interval endpoints.

Across all 24 comparisons, the saved lower RR endpoints exceed one,
the lower FAR endpoints exceed zero, and the lower intensity endpoints
are positive. This agreement supports the direction of the fitted
climate-state contrasts under the specified model. It does not provide
simultaneous coverage across comparisons or account for uncertainties
in product choice, spatial footprint, GMST, or tail specification. FAR
intervals are taken directly from the bootstrap FAR samples, rather
than obtained by transforming already rounded RR interval endpoints.

\section{Discussion}
The expanded results show a consistent positive location response to
GMST across all products, indices, and blocks examined. The magnitude
of the response varies substantially: annual daytime intensity change
for the 1976-like comparison ranges from 2.09 to
$4.03\ ^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$, while annual nighttime change ranges from
1.01 to $2.16\ ^{\circ}\mathrm{C}$. June point estimates are larger
within every product and index. Because records, footprints, and data
construction differ, these contrasts identify sensitivity among the
current analyses rather than isolating the effect of any one data choice.

The results also demonstrate that probability and intensity summaries
need to be reported together. CPC annual Tx3x combines the largest
intensity shift with the smallest annual daytime RR, while several
June fits produce infinite ratios from finite endpoints. FAR supplies
an expression of the relative risk difference with an upper bound of
one, but approaches
one rapidly as RR grows and can conceal large differences in the
underlying probabilities. Reporting FAR with RR, intensity change,
intervals, and fitted tail parameters makes these features visible.

The principal statistical limitation is the amount of information
available about the cooler-climate tail. Very large return periods and
near-endpoint estimates are controlled strongly by the fitted shape,
and the prevalence of infinite bootstrap ratios shows that this is not
only a property of one point estimate. The 200-replicate attribution
bootstrap also provides a limited basis for estimating tail percentiles,
especially where some fits fail. More replicates would clarify Monte
Carlo stability, but would not resolve model misspecification.

Further work should compare products over common fitting years, assess
the fixed-mask and GMST endpoint assumptions, and examine whether a
climate-dependent scale improves the fit. The current data products
share information and should not be averaged as independent estimates
without a model for their dependence. Similarly, separate Tx3x and
Tn3x fits do not characterize the joint occurrence of hot days and warm
nights. These extensions concern the robustness and scope of the
observational analysis described here, consistent with the broader
statistical issues discussed by Naveau et al.\ \cite{naveau2020}.

\section{Implementation and result provenance}
The numerical results are taken from the six product--variable output
directories in the \path{cpc_rr} project. The files ending in
\path{risk_ratios_PROVISIONAL.csv} provide point estimates, percentile
intervals, and retained bootstrap counts. The associated
\path{fits_and_bootstrap.rds} files provide fitted parameters and
bootstrap arrays, while \path{annual_extremes_and_gmst.csv} records
fitting years and inclusion. The separate \path{bootstrap_samples.csv}
files retain the individual replicate results. The tables were checked
against the saved fit objects and the percentile calculations used by
the implementation; no statistical model was refitted for tabulation.

Data preparation and distributional calculations use
\path{Code/config/paths.R}, \path{Code/helpers/workflow.R}, and
\path{Code/helpers/gev.R}. The GMST series is produced by
\path{Code/prepare_gmst.R}, and each product--variable script constructs
the corresponding indices and fits. The annual Tx3x figure is retained
from \path{figure7/figure7_tx3x_four_datasets.pdf}, with only its external
title removed for manuscript placement. Its summary file records
300 successful bootstrap fits for each of the four products.

The provisional designation reflects the implementation's data-version,
mask, GMST endpoint, and optimization choices; these results are not
claimed to reproduce the motivating report exactly. The tables report
products separately and use the existing successful-fit percentile
intervals. Reproduction requires the source data versions and the
original analysis settings in addition to these saved summaries.
